## How VCs Think (The Core Mental Model)

Every VC evaluation reduces to four questions, asked in this order:

1. **Is the market real and large enough?** A VC fund needs a 10x return on its winners to return the fund. That math requires a TAM that can support a company worth $100M–$1B+. For a $23M–$46M annual TAM (new-launch advisory alone), the question becomes: "Is this the wedge into something much larger?" The answer for PhysicianLabs is yes — the ongoing subscription TAM (existing independent practices) is an order of magnitude larger.

2. **Is the timing right — and why now?** Markets that "should exist" but don't always have a structural reason they haven't been built yet. The "why now" for PhysicianLabs is two-part: (a) burnout has made independent practice more intentional and the target customer more motivated, and (b) the productization infrastructure (Stripe, Notion, Zoom, Calendly) makes a concierge-to-SaaS model buildable by one founder with $15,000. The same product would have cost $500,000 to build in 2015.

3. **Does the founder have unusual, durable advantages?** VCs are betting on the person as much as the idea. The question is not "could someone build this?" — it's "why is this specific founder 10x more likely to build it than anyone else?" For PhysicianLabs: McKinsey advisory background (exact model match to concierge delivery), AWS/Amazon PM experience (scale pathway expertise), physician family network (warm access to the ICP), and the specific awareness that the gap exists (the founder has watched his brother navigate this).

4. **Can this compound into a defensible business?** Early-stage investors tolerate zero revenue. They do not tolerate a business that cannot develop a moat. The moat case for PhysicianLabs is the specialist referral network — built relationship by relationship through each engagement, and unreplicable by a large incumbent on a fast timeline.

### Marc Krejci — Pathfinder Foundry

**What he is likely evaluating:**
- Is the founder doing real customer discovery or pattern-matching to what they think is true?

## The Single Most Important VC Heuristic for This Stage

**"Default Alive" (Paul Graham, 2015):** At the pre-revenue stage, the question is not "will this work?" — it is "will this founder get to the point where we can find out?" The 30-day critical path (three warm referrals, first paid engagement target) is the clearest signal that the founder is Default Alive: they are not waiting for funding to start testing.

**The Power Law:** VCs need one investment to return the entire fund. A $23M–$46M annual TAM is a real business — but it is not a fund-returner on its own. The pitch to Carson Nye (and to any VC in this room) must include the expansion thesis. The ongoing subscription TAM (120,000+ existing independent practices at $200/month) is $290M/year. Say that number. It is what turns a small-market wedge into a fund-returnable thesis.

## The One Question That Will Come From Every VC in the Room

> "You have no paying customers. Why should I believe physicians will pay?"

**The answer that works:**
"You are right — that is the most important unvalidated assumption. I have not tried to pretend it is answered. It is the only job of the next 30 days. Three warm referrals are being requested this week from my physician network. The discovery calls use Mom Test discipline — past behavior and real financial pain, not forward-looking purchase intent. If five conversations end without a paying customer, I will have specific, actionable data on why. I will not spend 90 days building a product for a customer who will not pay."

**Why this answer works:** It does not defend the assumption. It demonstrates the methodology for testing it. VCs fund founders who know how to learn, not founders who have already figured everything out.
