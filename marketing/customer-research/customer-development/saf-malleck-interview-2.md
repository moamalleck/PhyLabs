## Cleaned Signals — Chronological

**[Opening] Strategic framing: pre-launch vs. post-launch ICP tension**

Before being asked, Saf surfaced the same structural problem with the pre-launch customer
thesis that Samir Master raised on May 28:

> "The people who understand the pain the most are not the people who have never started a
> clinic before. It's the people who have started the clinic already."

He went further — naming the specific implication for product strategy:

> "I could build something for pre-clinic owners, but then getting them to buy it is going to
> be a little bit tougher because they don't know the pain. So then I'm thinking, okay, maybe
> not pre-clinic owners. Maybe my main customer is somebody who's just launched a clinic in
> the past six months or in the past year and is starting to feel that pain."

**Significance:** This is now the second independent physician (after Samir) to reach the same
conclusion without prompting. Both have run practices for 10–16 years. The convergence is a
strong signal that the pre-launch proof-of-value problem is structural, not anecdotal.

---

**[~8 min] EMR selection: familiarity bias, not rational evaluation**

On how physicians choose their EMR:

> "It's biased towards what you've been exposed to already before, and then also what your
> colleagues are using. I've been exposed to Acuro. Most people have been exposed to Acuro in
> Canada. And so a lot of us are biased towards that."

He added the Canadian healthcare context:

> "In a Canadian healthcare system where remuneration for services are very low, people aren't
> looking for the best stuff. Because it's not quality-driven, it's quantity-driven. People
> just choose the closest thing that they've experienced somebody else use before."

The three setup non-negotiables, named directly: **location, secretary, EMR** — in that order.

---

**[~12 min] Incorporation/tax pain — the mechanic, fully explained**

Saf described the specific false belief that delays incorporation — the clearest articulation
of this pain point in any interview to date:

> "Some of my colleagues, they're young and they're like: once I pay down all my debts, then
> I will incorporate."

He explained why this belief forms:

> "What you're taught is: if you still have debt to pay, you have to withdraw it from the
> company. And so what you're doing is paying for the company and then also paying taxes that
> you have to withdraw to pay your debt off. Eventually you're actually losing in general.
> That's what you're taught. And so what they say is: once you've done paying all your debts,
> then start the incorporation."

The reason this belief persists:

> "Nobody guides you through this process. The accountant does a little bit, the bank does a
> little bit, you have to do your own little bit. And that is actually a very disorganized
> process that nobody tells you."

The personal consequence:

> "We've gone into debt paying tax. We're actually in a lot of debt right now, purely because
> of how horrible I set it up."

The grey-area deductions his accountant is teaching him now:
- Car depreciation: structured as 80% business use by choosing clinic location near home (so
  secondary work sites — hospital, etc. — become the deductible commute)
- Coffee, meals, any conversation tangentially related to work → business expense
- The entire "traveling conference" model: attend an online session, travel to Hawaii, write
  off the trip as a conference expense

> "I knew I had to get incorporated from the start, but I was working so much — only home at
> 2am every night for the first three to four years. So I actually went to the banks and tried
> to get information. But to actually do it, you have to sit down and go through many different
> things."

**[~20 min] Willingness to pay — the clearest WTP signal in the dataset**

The direct WTP exchange, verbatim:

> **Ash:** "How much would you have paid for [incorporation/tax guidance]? Would it be a
> one-time payment or something you'd pay monthly?"
>
> **Saf:** "In retrospect, I would have paid $10,000."
>
> **Ash:** "Before, how much would people pay before not knowing the amount of pain?"
>
> **Saf:** "That's the problem. People don't know the pain until they've gone through it...
> But I think $1,000 — that's reasonable. But even then, people — just doctors don't want to
> pay anything. Professionals don't want to pay anything. That's the issue. But if you frame
> it like: look, this is going to save you literally $100,000 a year minimum — you could get
> people to pay."

Ash then proposed the retainer model ($1–2K upfront, quarterly reviews, value demonstrated
via savings shown). Saf responded positively.

**The WTP summary:**

| Frame | Amount |
|---|---|
| Retrospective (knowing the outcome) | $10,000 |
| Prospective (before feeling pain) | $1,000 |
| Value unlock framing | "Save $100K/year minimum" |
| Saf's general characterisation | "Doctors don't want to pay anything" |

**[~22 min] Existing competitors: Dr. Phan/Vu — medical finance webinars, Toronto**

Saf mentioned a doctor he knows in Toronto who runs financial education webinars and "traveling
conferences" for physicians:

> "There are people that are trying to do webinars on finance for doctors... I know one of the
> guys that tries to sell that stuff, doing talks all the time. That's his angle: medical
> finance. But I'd like to do one of his courses or just go to one of his talks to see what
> he says."

The doctor's name: **Phan or Vu** (phonetic — exact spelling unclear from transcript). Based
in Toronto (possibly California now — transcript unclear). Connected to Saf via a WhatsApp
group. He runs the "traveling conference" model: attendees log in to an online session while
traveling anywhere (Hawaii, Miami), then write off the travel as a conference expense.

> "He's tried to start like 20 businesses and a lot of them have failed. But he's very much
> hardcore into like the business side of health care."

**Significance:** This is a named existing competitor/adjacent player in the physician-finance
education space in Canada. Saf has not attended his courses but would. He is a distribution
reference point and potentially a channel partner or competitor benchmark.

**[~25 min] Distribution: Facebook doctor groups and med student bodies**

Two distribution channels surfaced:

1. **Facebook private doctor groups** — Saf was invited to one by a colleague. He doesn't know
   the group name or how to access it for outreach, but described it as an active community
   where physicians discuss practice and business topics.

2. **Med school student bodies / residency programs** — Saf confirmed residents finishing
   training are the ideal timing for behaviour change:

> "Those people have the least access to information versus people who are a little bit further
> into their career — they have access to information. They can ask a colleague. They can ask me."

> "Students as they're coming out and even residents as they're coming out of their training —
> those are probably the best people to actually get a hold of. Because that's when you want
> the best change and advice."

Ash mentioned he is already in conversations with contacts connected to University of Washington
medical school groups. Saf endorsed this direction.

---

**[~27 min] Why money is a taboo topic in medicine**

Saf articulated the cultural reason business and money discussions are absent from medical
training:

> "In medicine, if somebody gives you that financial lens, then what you're saying is every
> job that they're doing, every little service that they provide, it's seen through the lens
> of a financial gain. And that's not what medicine should be."

> "Not a single person has ever told me anything [about business/tax optimization]. They'll
> say: save on tax, TFSA, RRSP. But they'll never talk about the nitty-gritty of the business
> and the money aspect — like, this is what you do to maximize here and there. Never. Not part
> of the culture."

**Significance:** This is the cultural mechanism that sustains the information vacuum.
It is not just negligence or oversight — it is an active norm. "Medicine is not for money"
is the implicit contract that prevents the knowledge from being passed down. This framing
could be a powerful element in the pitch narrative.
