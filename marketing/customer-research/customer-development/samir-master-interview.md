## Cleaned Signals — Chronological

**[01:50–04:40] Samir's background: non-linear physician with deep venture/enterprise credentials**

Before any substantive discussion, context was established. Samir grew up in Iowa with immigrant
physician parents, programmed in BASIC/Pascal/Assembler before high school, went directly from
high school to Northwestern (2 years undergrad) then medical school, took a year off for Kellogg
MBA, completed internal medicine residency in Seattle (1993), dermatology fellowship at Michigan
(1995), and then — instead of practicing — spent approximately six years in venture at the
Kleiner Perkins era, served on the Netflix board, joined Novartis in Switzerland as Global VP
of Business Development and Licensing, and only returned to practice dermatology "at the age
of 40." He has run Dermatology Arts for approximately 16 years since.

> "I went back to do two more years of training in Dermatology at the age of 40, and then came
> back and started my practice."

**[05:07] Role reversal trigger — Samir redirects to seeing the pitch**

After Ash noted interest in Samir's background, Samir asked to see what Ash is building, citing
Compass as directly relevant.

> "Tell me what you're trying to solve — because I think Compass is directly relevant to what
> I hope you're trying to do."

This was the moment Ash handed over the agenda. Ash then gave a ~3-minute product pitch (two
phases: pre-launch setup, post-launch decision intelligence), which Samir listened to and then
responded to as the expert rather than the subject.

**[12:06–15:40] Ash pitches; Samir frames the SMB market gap**

Samir's first substantive response to the pitch confirmed the problem and began reframing the
market structure:

> "I know a lot of doctors — not a single one, you are probably the first — understands a P&L
> enough to be confident in it, and knows what to do with a balance sheet, even though their
> accountant creates it every year for their tax purposes. That's what I want to solve for."

> "That's a pain point that is actually ending up causing private clinics to close downstream,
> but also causing fear and anxiety with experienced doctors who want to have more agency."

**[18:41–23:00] The SMB market structure — validated from the inside**

Samir walked through the market architecture unprompted: large health systems are Epic customers
(Swedish, UW, Overlake, MultiCare, Kaiser in Seattle); Epic will not sell to independent
practices; the entire SMB ambulatory market has been "entirely ignored by software" because
CAC/service cost economics don't work for vendors at the per-practice level.

> "The entire SMB market has been entirely ignored. By the software industry. Mainly because
> they perceive incremental value to being too low, and the cost of sales and the cost of
> ongoing service are too high for them."

> "I suspect that [frontier labs and enterprise software vendors] will continue to be [absent
> from SMB]. Despite that — how many independent physicians are there? 300,000 plus? Probably
> half a million."

He then walked through the TAM live using AI (Google Gemini), arriving at $250–300B in SMB
ambulatory practice revenues, ~$12B software SAM at 5% spend — consistent with Ash's own
bottom-up model.

**[25:28–28:16] ModMed: the dominant derm EMR is 16 years old and still inadequate**

Samir has used ModMed (modernizing medicine) for 16 years — the longest-tenured customer in
any interview to date on any EMR. His assessment:

> "This was the only dermatology-centric EHR. It was by far and away the best, even though at
> the time it was a D-minus product for my needs — and there is just no alternative. That's
> crazy."

> "How much have they advanced in the last 16 years? D-minus to C-plus, maybe B-plus, you tell
> me. No, no, no — that's about this much. Maybe even worse. Maybe it's a D, I mean — yeah,
> it's still a C-minus."

He then listed each capability his practice requires that ModMed does NOT include: voice agent
($1,000/month from a third party), online scheduling (not included), eligibility verification
(not included), copay collection (not included), AI scribe ($1,000/month extra). Each requires
a separate vendor, separate API integration, and often sharing dummy login credentials.

> "Every one of these things that I want, I actually have to add on to this myself at my cost,
> and I have to figure out who the best vendor is, and I have to figure out how I'm going to
> connect in either via API or sometimes we just give them dummy credentials — I pretend as if
> so-and-so software is one of my employees."

**[28:16–28:40] Admin overhead: $200K/year benchmark**

Unprompted, Samir disclosed his full administrative headcount:

> "Right now I have five people answering the phone for me. I have two or three people at the
> front desk. I have two people on billing. I have three medical assistants that just work for
> me alone. That's $200,000 a year."

He added: "And there's no way too [much]. No wonder we can't be very profitable." He noted his
EBITDA is "probably triple" the average derm practice — implying the average is substantially
below his — attributing it to "simple business" disciplines that most practice owners don't apply.

**[33:17–40:00] Samir's own product vision: Rippling meets Compass for outpatient medicine**

Samir described the system he is building: a layer above the EMR that serves as a unified system
of record, connecting intake → patient management → revenue cycle, and that plugs into ModMed,
Epic, and Athena alike. He framed it as analogous to Compass for real estate agents — a platform
for people who "are not computer people" but need end-to-end workflow intelligence.

> "A combination between Compass and Rippling. A system of record that now spans the entire
> workflow — from 'I need a dermatologist' to 'my bill is fully paid' and I already have an
> automatic reminder."

He confirmed he has:
- A complete PRD
- 25 technical design documents (TDDs)
- ~100 mapped workflows from his own practice
- One local developer
- Offshore development capability
- A target of shipping to his own practice "probably in the first half of next year"
- Relationships with "a couple of large, 100-plus dermatology practices that are all on ModMed
  and looking to shift"

**[40:00–42:15] Insurance reimbursement discrimination: $115 vs $290**

The single most quotable data point of the session. Samir disclosed his contracted rate for a
new patient visit and compared it to what Swedish Hospital receives for the same patient:

> "For a Blue Cross Blue Shield plan — a new patient, low complexity — our contracted rate is
> less than $115. Guess what Swedish gets? $290."

> "If I worked for Swedish and I got a percentage of collections, I'd be millions, right."

> "There is a systematic collusion, so to speak, against small and independent practices — to
> force them out of business. Because my rent costs are the same as Swedish. My administrative
> costs are the same. My labor costs are the same. My supplies are probably even more expensive
> because I don't have negotiating power."

He validated the market consolidation data: "In 2012, 60% of all medical practices were
independent. Now it's 40%." Live AI check confirmed 42.2%.

**[44:27–47:42] Competitor and product landscape assessment**

Samir reviewed Ash's competitor slide, noting that most listed companies (clinical works, Athena
Health, modernizing medicine, newer AI entrants) were built 15+ years ago on government EHR
digitization subsidies and have not been meaningfully updated.

> "What other software do you use today that is 15 years old? Maybe Microsoft Excel — and that,
> hopefully, is also entirely refreshed."

On the newer AI point solutions (voice agents etc.):

> "I interviewed like 20 of them in December, January. Again, a thousand dollars per month.
> Is that what I want to do? Some of them are like, yeah, I can get down to 500 for a location.
> But you know it's nonsense. Why are we adding all of these things on when there can just be
> one system of record?"

He noted a potential future strategic move: if multiple independent practices adopt a shared
system of record, they become a **Clinically Integrated Network (CIN)** with collective
negotiating power against insurance payers — moving reimbursement rates toward the health
system tier.

**[57:26–59:12] Samir's cautions: the two hardest problems for Ash's go-to-market**

Unprompted, Samir named two structural difficulties with the pre-launch (Launch Gauntlet) thesis:

1. **Generational shift toward employment:**
> "There is a generational shift among people that want to work and clock out versus people
> that want to run their own. I've noticed a huge shift in the last 18 years, even amongst
> dermatologists. Now you can get paid at a large system and make more money than I can offer."

2. **Distribution difficulty for pre-launch customers:**
> "Don't underestimate the difficulty in identifying the target customer. There are probably
> 500 people that are going to finish training at UW this year, in about four weeks. Do you
> know what they're all going to do? They're all spread out across 30 different departments,
> five different hospitals. How are you actually going to find them even in a town as
> concentrated as Seattle?"

3. **Proof-of-value timing problem:**
> "By definition, you won't have the ability to prove your product until it's too late, right?
> I just started my practice — how do you prove value two years later?"

**[01:00:35–01:02:30] Willingness to pay: doctors are "exceedingly cheap"**

Samir was direct about SMB physician WTP:

> "People in my situation are likely generally exceedingly cheap."

> "Just be mindful of how hard it is to even get five thousand dollars."

He then challenged Ash to ask his brother Seth (a doctor Ash had done consulting work for):
"Would you give me twenty thousand dollars to do this project for you?" — implying that even
family relationships don't guarantee easy payment. He noted Ash had identified $200K in savings
for his brother but had charged only $10K — and flagged this as a meaningful WTP signal.

**[01:02:30–01:07:10] Business model and target segment guidance**

Samir's strongest strategic recommendation was to target physician *groups*, not solo
practitioners:

> "You're also going to be driven to larger groups. Once there are 30 doctors, no one person
> is paying [the bill]. There's an admin that's deciding whether to give you a hundred-thousand-
> dollar check."

He named specific local physician associations: **Pro Alliance** and **Western Washington
Medical Group**, both of which he noted were "losing pods of doctors" — groups that split off
to form independent practices, representing warm pre-launch ICP clusters.

**[01:04:00–01:06:20] Business model critique: Netflix analogy**

Samir recounted his Netflix board experience (DVD-era business model transformation) as a
framework for thinking about PhysicianLabs' model:

> "As you're thinking about your approach — my strongest guidance would be: think about your
> business model. Because both of them are well-known, well-understood. You're not trying to
> reinvent the wheel. But I think both of them are difficult, non-scalable business models."

He identified consulting projects and point solutions as the two failure modes, and suggested
thinking about recurring revenue tied to practice performance improvement as the direction that
scales.
