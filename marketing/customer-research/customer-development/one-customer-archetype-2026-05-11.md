## PRIMARY ARCHETYPE (Active June 2026): Marcus

**Marcus Chen, MD** -- Interventional Cardiologist, 11 years post-residency, currently employed at a large regional hospital system in the Pacific Northwest. He has decided to leave and open an independent or concierge practice within the next 12 months. He knows the medicine. He does not know the business.

> Marcus is a composite built from interview evidence (Sameer Gafoor, Saf Malleck, Samir Master), the ICP decision document (May 31, 2026), and the Reddit prospect discovery run (June 1, 2026). All data points below are grounded in those sources.

---

### Marcus's Full Story

Marcus graduated from a top-20 medical school, completed his residency and fellowship at a prestigious academic centre, and has spent the last eleven years building a reputation as one of the best interventional cardiologists in his region. His patients know him. His colleagues respect him. His hospital system knows this too -- and uses it.

He is paid $380,000 a year. His department bills over $2.1 million annually on his caseload. He has never seen that number. He found it once in a public filing and did not sleep well that night.

The bureaucracy has compounded over the years. Prior authorizations that take three days to resolve. An EMR that was chosen by an administrator who has never seen a patient. A scheduling system optimised for throughput, not care. A noncompete clause that covers a 25-mile radius and 18 months. He signed it without negotiating because he was a resident and did not know he could.

He is done.

He has told his wife. He has told one trusted colleague who made the jump two years ago and now runs a concierge cardiology practice with 180 members and a three-month waitlist. That colleague told him: "The first year is the hardest thing you will ever do. Not clinically -- operationally. Nobody tells you anything. You just have to figure it out."

Marcus has started a list on his phone. It has 23 items. He does not know if it is complete. He does not know what order to do them in. He does not know which ones have hard deadlines that will cost him money if missed.

---

### The Financial Blind Spot

Marcus earns well and manages his personal finances competently. He has a brokerage account, a 457(b), and a mortgage he is paying down ahead of schedule.

He knows almost nothing about how to structure a physician-owned practice entity.

He does not know:
- Whether to form a Professional Corporation, PLLC, or S-corp in his state -- and that the answer depends on his state's corporate practice of medicine laws
- That electing S-corp tax treatment requires filing IRS Form 2553 within 75 days of entity formation -- and that missing this window costs him one full tax year of savings
- That paying himself a below-market salary to maximise distributions will trigger IRS scrutiny in his income bracket and requires a defensible "reasonable compensation" analysis
- That his hospital noncompete may restrict not just where he practices but who he can hire and which payers he can contract with
- That credentialing as an independent cardiologist takes 90 to 150 days per payer -- and that he must start before he gives notice, not after

He will find out most of this the hard way, or through the colleague who already made the jump. The colleague is helpful but busy. The answers are inconsistent. The timeline is unforgiving.

> "The amount I'm losing by not having this structured correctly from Day 1 is not a small number. And I won't find out what it is until it's too late to fix it." -- composite from Saf Malleck and Samir Master interviews

---

### What Marcus Aspires To

Marcus's mental image of what he is building is specific. A boutique cardiology practice -- 300 to 400 concierge members at $3,000 to $5,000 per year -- where he sees patients he has chosen to take on, at a pace that allows him to do the job the way he was trained to do it. Same-day appointments. No prior auth fights. Direct relationships with patients who value what he offers.

The financial version: within three years, his practice generates $1.2 million in gross revenue. After a physician-optimised entity structure, his effective tax rate is materially lower than it is today. He has a retained earnings account growing year over year. He is not dependent on a hospital system for his livelihood.

He is not trying to get rich. He has enough. He is trying to own his own work, practice medicine at the standard he trained for, and stop subsidising a system that does not share his values.

---

### How Marcus Makes Decisions

Marcus is analytically rigorous and professionally cautious. He does not make decisions without evidence. He does not trust cold outreach. He trusts:

1. **A peer who has done it.** His concierge cardiology colleague is the single most influential voice in his decision-making. One specific data point from that colleague outweighs ten articles.
2. **Structured expertise, not general advice.** He has consulted a general business attorney and found the advice too broad to be actionable. He is looking for someone who has done this specifically for physicians, not someone who has done it for small businesses generally.
3. **A clear ROI calculation.** He will pay for guidance if the financial case is explicit. "Save $200,000 in preventable tax losses over three years" is a sentence he will act on. "Optimise your practice structure" is not.

### What Marcus Says Yes To

1. A peer or trusted colleague recommended the product or person specifically -- not generically
2. The offer is framed around a concrete financial outcome he can verify ("physicians in your structure typically recover $150K to $400K in the first three years")
3. The person offering it clearly understands the physician-specific constraints (corporate practice of medicine, noncompete structure, payer credentialing, Medicare opt-in/out)
4. The price is positioned as obviously less than the cost of getting it wrong

### What Marcus Says No To

1. Anything that feels like a generic small-business product dressed in medical language
2. Cold outreach that does not reference something specific he said or did
3. A price that requires him to evaluate value rather than having the value made obvious
4. Anything requiring a long evaluation process -- he is busy and will abandon it

## Archetype Name: Jordan

**Jordan Park, DPT** -- Doctor of Physical Therapy, 3 years post-graduation, currently employed at a hospital outpatient clinic in Seattle. She has decided to open her first independent PT practice. She does not yet know what she does not know.

> Jordan is a composite built from interview evidence (Saf Malleck, Shaun Cowan, Hannan Qureshi), mentor guidance (David Jorjani, Chelsea Parkman), and the PT segment analysis scoring 23/25 -- the highest of all three beachhead segments. All data points below are grounded in those sources.

---

## Jordan's Full Story

### The Beginning: A Good Clinician in a Bad System

Jordan grew up in Shoreline, just north of Seattle, the daughter of a nurse and a civil engineer. She was a competitive soccer player through high school, tore her ACL at seventeen, and spent eight months in rehabilitation. She does not remember the injury. She remembers her physical therapist.

She remembers the way that person -- a woman in her early thirties who worked out of a small private clinic -- explained exactly what was happening inside her knee, what the recovery would look like week by week, and what the goal was for each session. She remembers feeling like someone was in charge of the process. She decided then that she wanted to do that for people.

She graduated from the University of Washington with a degree in kinesiology, was accepted to the DPT program at Pacific University in Oregon, and finished her doctorate at twenty-five. Her student loan balance at graduation: $118,000. She had four job offers. She chose the one at a hospital-affiliated outpatient clinic in Bellevue because it offered the best caseload variety and she liked the supervising PT. The salary was $82,000.

She is now thirty. The supervising PT she liked left eighteen months ago. The clinic hired a new manager who came up through administration, not clinical practice. There are productivity quotas: twelve to fourteen patients per day. She is good at hitting them. She does not like what hitting them has done to the quality of care she gives.

---

### The Moment She Decided

It happened on a Tuesday in February.

She was eating lunch at her desk, scrolling through the Physical Therapy Owners Club Facebook group -- a habit she had developed over the past year, somewhere between professional development and escapism. A post caught her eye. A physical therapist in Portland had written a thread about her first year running her own clinic. She described the anxiety of the first month, the first insurance payment, the first hire. She also described her revenue in Year 2: $390,000 in collections from a solo clinic with one part-time PTA.

Jordan sat with that for a moment. She was billing the same number of patients per week. She knew what her employer was collecting on her caseload. She knew what she was being paid.

She put her lunch down, opened a new note on her phone, and wrote: "Things I need to figure out to open my own clinic." She had six bullet points by the time her break ended. Over the following three weeks, the list grew to thirty-one. She kept finding new items to add and had no way to know if the list was complete, in the right order, or missing anything critical.

She told one colleague, a PT she trusted. The colleague said: "I've been thinking about it too. Have you talked to anyone who has actually done it?" She had not. She messaged two PTs she had met at an APTA state chapter event who were in private practice. One replied with encouragement and a list of EHR recommendations. The other replied with a caution about how hard the first year was. Neither had any specific guidance on what to do first.

She did not feel informed. She felt like she was looking at a map with no scale and no compass.

---

### What a Typical Week Looks Like

Jordan arrives at the clinic at 7:45 AM. She reviews her schedule for the day -- twelve patients. The first three are post-surgical orthopedic cases she knows well. The fourth is a new evaluation she has not had time to prep for. She pulls up the referral notes while the patient checks in.

By noon she has seen six patients, written four SOAP notes in WebPT, and fielded two phone calls from the front desk about prior authorization requests. One patient's auth expired mid-episode. She has to write a letter of medical necessity before the end of the day or the patient's next visit is on hold.

She eats at her desk again. She opens PTOC. Today's most active thread: someone asking whether to go insurance-based or cash-pay at launch. Forty-seven replies. She reads all of them.

She finishes the day at 5:30 PM after seeing all twelve patients and completing all but two of her SOAP notes. She does the remaining two from her couch at 9:15 PM after dinner.

On Saturday morning she does her "clinic research hour." She has a folder in her Chrome browser called "Opening a Clinic." It contains forty-two bookmarked tabs: CAQH enrollment guides, an article about PLLC vs. S-Corp from a general small business blog, a Reddit thread about credentialing timelines in Washington State, a webinar recording about EHR selection, and a YouTube video from a PT business coach about marketing your new clinic. She has watched portions of most of them. She has not finished any of them. There is no connecting thread between them. None of them covers all the steps. None of them covers any of the steps in the right order.

Her partner asks how the research is going. She says: "There's a lot to learn." She does not say that the real feeling is that the more she reads, the less she knows what to do first.

---

### How She Makes Decisions

Jordan is analytically comfortable. She is used to clinical reasoning -- hypothesis, evidence, differential, plan. She applies the same structure to business decisions, except that she does not have the equivalent of a clinical education to draw from. She is reading the textbooks while also trying to treat patients.

Her decision-making process for anything significant follows a pattern:

**Step 1: Awareness.** Something surfaces a problem she did not know existed or had not quantified. This happens most often through PTOC threads, APTA state chapter conversations, or a peer describing a specific experience.

**Step 2: Research.** She opens Chrome and searches. She finds partial information from multiple sources. The information is inconsistent. She cannot tell what applies to her specific situation (solo PT, Washington State, insurance-based practice, starting from scratch).

**Step 3: Social validation.** She posts a question in PTOC or DMs a PT she trusts. She waits for a reply with direct experience, not theory. If a peer gives her a specific answer -- "I used X, here's what happened" -- she acts. If she gets only general opinions, she stalls.

**Step 4: The decision.** Either she acts based on peer validation (fast, within 48 hours of a good peer response) or she adds the item to her Notes app list under "things to figure out" and it stays there for weeks.

**What she does not do:** She does not respond to cold advertising. She does not click sponsored content. She will not book a demo with a company she has not heard of from someone she trusts. She is not cynical -- she simply does not have time to evaluate things she has no social proof for, and she defaults to protecting her limited bandwidth.

**What changes her behavior:** A PTOC thread where someone describes a specific, concrete outcome. Not "this tool is great," but "I was about to open my clinic and didn't realize credentialing would take four months. I started the process three months late and had zero insurance income for my first seven months. I wish someone had told me sooner." That sentence stops her scrolling. She reads the whole thread. If the product being discussed passes the peer credibility test, she buys within two days.

---

### The Financial Blind Spot

Jordan has a working knowledge of personal finance. She has a Roth IRA. She pays extra toward her student loans each month. She uses a budget app and knows her fixed expenses within fifty dollars.

She knows almost nothing about small business financial structure for a professional services provider.

She does not know:
- That she should form a Professional Limited Liability Company (PLLC) in Washington State -- not a standard LLC -- before she begins practicing as an independent provider
- That she can elect S-Corporation tax treatment for her PLLC once her net income exceeds approximately $40,000 per year, saving her roughly $5,000 to $12,000 annually in self-employment taxes at her projected income level
- That paying herself a "reasonable salary" from the corporation and taking the remainder as distributions is the core mechanism by which physician-owned clinics reduce their tax burden legally
- That every month she operates without this structure in place is a month she is paying 15.3% self-employment tax on income she could be distributing as a pass-through dividend
- That the decision to incorporate retroactively is not available -- the clock starts when she starts billing

She will find this out eventually. The most likely path: a colleague mentions it at a PTOC meetup, or she accidentally hires an accountant who specializes in healthcare professionals rather than a general practitioner who files her personal taxes once a year. That conversation will happen somewhere between Month 6 and Year 3 of her clinic's operation.

When it does, her reaction will be the same as Saf Malleck's: a feeling that is equal parts relief at finally knowing and grief at how long it took.

*"The amount of tax I'm paying is nauseating. Had I known this five years ago, the house I'd be living in would be double the size."*

Jordan does not yet know she is one to three years away from saying something almost identical.

---

### The Credentialing Time Bomb

Jordan knows that she needs to get on insurance panels. She has a vague sense that this takes "a few weeks." She plans to apply when she signs her lease, which she expects to happen about six weeks before she opens.

She does not know:
- That the average credentialing timeline for new solo providers in Washington State is 90 to 120 days per payer, not weeks
- That CAQH profile completion is a prerequisite and takes one to two weeks to verify
- That each payer (Regence, Premera, Aetna, UHC, L&I, Medicaid, Medicare) is a separate application with separate paperwork, separate processing timelines, and separate follow-up requirements
- That she must start every application before she opens, not after -- and ideally before she signs a lease
- That 65% of new clinics open without full payer enrollment, meaning their first patients are on a cash-pay basis the owner is uncomfortable charging
- That the 30 to 90 day lag between first patient visit and first insurance payment means she needs 3 to 6 months of operating capital from day one

She will open her clinic. She will see her first patients at cash rates she discounts out of guilt ("I don't have my insurance yet, I'll charge you less until I do"). She will watch her savings drop by $4,000 to $6,000 per month while her credentialing applications are in process. Sometime around Month 3, standing in her clinic at 6 PM after seeing eight patients and earning approximately $400 of take-home cash for the day, she will feel the specific panic that 92% of new clinic owners experience: the fear that she made a catastrophic mistake.

She will get through it. Clinics that make it past Month 6 usually survive. But she will carry the scar of those months for years, and she will tell every PT she knows: "Start your credentialing before you do anything else."

---

### What She Aspires To

Jordan has a mental image of what she is building. It is not abstract.

In this image, she has a clinic with her name on the door -- Jordan Park Physical Therapy, or something cleaner. It has two treatment rooms and a small gym floor. She sees six to eight patients per day rather than twelve, but each session is forty-five minutes and thorough rather than thirty minutes and pressured. She knows every patient's name, their sport or activity, their three-month goal. She finishes her notes before she drives home. She is not eating lunch at her desk.

In this image she has one or two other PTs working under her within three years. She is the clinic they wanted to join because the culture is good and the ownership model is transparent. She is not running a pyramid scheme.

The financial version of this image: she is paying off her student loans at an accelerated pace, has a retained earnings account inside her professional corporation growing year over year, and is not panicking about a bad month because she has four months of operating capital in reserve.

She is not trying to get rich. She is trying to build something she owns, practice medicine the way she wants to practice it, and not feel like she is being extracted by the system she trained in.

---

### The Moment She Would Buy

It is a Sunday evening. Jordan is in bed with her phone, scrolling PTOC. She sees a post from a PT in Tacoma she does not know personally but whose content she has seen before -- thoughtful, specific, credible. The post reads:

*"For anyone opening a clinic in the next 12 months: I almost made a mistake that would have cost me 5 months of income. I was planning to apply for insurance panels after I signed my lease. I had no idea that credentialing takes 3 to 4 months in WA State. PhysicianLabs caught it in their setup checklist and told me to apply immediately. I signed my lease in October and I had my first Regence payment by February. If I had waited until after I signed, I would have been cash-pay only until spring. That checklist alone was worth every dollar."*

Jordan reads it three times. She clicks through to the PhysicianLabs website. She reads the pricing page. She opens the PTOC thread and searches for other mentions of the name. She finds two more posts -- both specific, both from PTs who opened clinics in the past year.

She pulls up her Notes app and looks at her "things to figure out" list. She sees "figure out insurance credentialing" sitting at item number 7, with no date and no action next to it.

She opens the PhysicianLabs website again and clicks the button that says "Start your Launch Checklist."

It is 10:43 PM. She has work in the morning. She fills out the intake form anyway.

## Section 5: Pain Points (Ranked by Intensity)

### Pain 1 -- Financial structure ignorance (CRITICAL -- Saf signal)
She does not know the difference between a PLLC, an S-Corp election, and a professional corporation. She does not know that the timing of incorporation has tax consequences measured in the tens of thousands of dollars per year. She will likely form an entity when her attorney or accountant tells her to -- which may be months or years after she should have. The cost of this delay is silent and invisible until a good accountant surfaces it.

Evidence: Saf Malleck -- *"The amount of tax I'm paying is nauseating. Had I known this five years ago, the house I'd be living in would be double the size."*

### Pain 2 -- The credentialing time bomb (HIGH)
Jordan does not know that payer credentialing takes 90 to 120 days per payer in WA State. She is planning to open her clinic and expects insurance to flow within a few weeks. She will open with zero payer contracts active, see her first patients at a cash-pay rate she is uncomfortable charging, and spend months 2 to 4 watching her savings evaporate while waiting for Regence and Premera to process her application.

Evidence: 65% of new clinics open without full payer enrollment; 92% hit a cash crisis at months 2 to 4.

### Pain 3 -- No map for the 50 steps (HIGH)
She knows there are steps. She does not know all of them, does not know the order, and does not know which ones have hard deadlines. Credentialing (must start Day 1), NPI group vs. individual (must decide before billing), Medicare opt-in vs. opt-out (permanent decision), CAQH profile completion (blocks credentialing if incomplete) -- none of these were covered in her DPT program.

Evidence: Problem statement core finding; Saf -- "The setup is manageable with the right colleague. I had that. Most people don't."

### Pain 4 -- Patient pipeline anxiety (MEDIUM-HIGH)
She is good at treating patients she already has. She does not know how to get new patients when she opens. She has never run a Google Business Profile, responded to a patient review, or contacted an insurance network directory to update her listing. Hannan Qureshi confirmed: in competitive urban markets (Seattle qualifies), Google SEO, reviews, and insurance directory visibility are the three channels that actually drive new patients.

### Pain 5 -- Medical secretary hiring (MEDIUM -- Saf signal)
She needs one competent, EMR-trained medical secretary before she can see her first patient. She has no idea how to hire one. She will post on Indeed, receive 500 applications, and spend 60 hours screening them while also trying to keep her current job. Saf screened 2,000 to 4,000 applications over two hiring rounds and spent 6 months finding a good hire the second time.

### Pain 6 -- Working capital gap (MEDIUM)
She does not know how much money she needs to survive before her first insurance check arrives (30 to 90 days after first patient). She is planning to use her savings and a small line of credit. She has not modeled the cash flow gap by payer or projected the operating capital requirement.
