# PhysicianLabs — Physician Practice Launch Checklist
**Version:** 1.0
**Date:** 2026-05-19
**Scope:** Idea to launched clinic — digital first, then physical

---

## How This Checklist Works

Two tracks. One sequence.

**Track 1 (Steps 1-130 approx.):** Get legal, get credentialed, get funded, and launch your digital practice first. This generates your first revenue, proves your workflows, and funds your physical expansion — before you sign a lease or hire a single front-desk employee.

**Track 2 (Steps 131-238):** Once your digital clinic is live and cash-flowing, open your physical doors. You arrive with tested workflows, enrolled payers, a patient base, and real revenue data to back your lease negotiation.

**Critical sequencing rule:** Credentialing is the longest lead item on this entire list. It takes 90-180 days and must start on Day 1, in parallel with everything else. Late credentialing start is the single most common cause of the months 2-4 working capital crisis.

## PHASE 0: DECIDE YOUR PRACTICE OUTLINE
*Commit to the vision. Define the model. Recruit your advisors.*

**The Vision and Model**
- [ ] 1. Define your practice model: solo, group, concierge, Direct Primary Care (DPC), hybrid, telehealth-first, or subspecialty focused
- [ ] 2. Define the services you will offer at launch vs. services you plan to add later
- [ ] 3. Define your target patient population: specialty, demographics, geography
- [ ] 4. Choose your payment model: fee-for-service, DPC/subscription, cash-pay, hybrid — or a combination
- [ ] 5. Decide your launch sequence: digital-first (telehealth) then physical, or simultaneous
- [ ] 6. Research the Corporate Practice of Medicine (CPOM) doctrine in your state — it determines who can own your entity

**Personal Financial Readiness**
- [ ] 7. Assess personal savings runway: how many months of personal living expenses can you cover before practice revenue begins?
- [ ] 8. Inventory existing debt: student loans, personal loans, mortgage — model the impact on cash flow
- [ ] 9. Determine the income gap: calculate how long you can go without your hospital or employed salary
- [ ] 10. Set a personal financial floor: the minimum monthly draw you need to sustain household through the launch phase

**Assemble Your Advisory Team — Do This Before Anything Else**
- [ ] 11. Engage a healthcare-specialized attorney (entity formation, STARK Law, Anti-Kickback, contracts, lease review)
- [ ] 12. Engage a healthcare CPA or accountant (entity tax strategy, financial modeling, quarterly estimated taxes)
- [ ] 13. Engage a credentialing specialist or service (CAQH management, payer applications, follow-up)
- [ ] 14. Identify a practice management advisor, consultant, or mentor (practice startup experience is essential)

**Market Validation**
- [ ] 15. Research patient demand in your target geography: population density, unmet specialty need, insurance coverage rates by zip code
- [ ] 16. Analyze the competitive landscape: number of competing practices, hospital system dominance, referral patterns
- [ ] 17. Identify your top potential referral sources (PCPs, specialists, hospitals, care coordinators)
- [ ] 18. Confirm payer mix for your specialty in your market (which insurance plans dominate, what they reimburse)

## PHASE 1: SET UP YOUR CLINIC
*Entity. EIN. Banking. Insurance. You are a business now.*

**Entity Formation**
> WARNING: Wrong entity type or wrong incorporation timing costs $200,000 to $400,000 in avoidable taxes. Do not skip or rush this step.

- [ ] 19. Select entity structure with healthcare attorney AND CPA together:
  - PC (Professional Corporation) or PLLC (Professional Limited Liability Company) — required in most states for licensed physicians
  - S-Corp election (IRC Section 1362) — can be layered onto a PC or PLLC for pass-through tax treatment
  - The right answer depends on your state's CPOM rules, your income level, and your growth plan
- [ ] 20. Draft and file articles of incorporation or organization with state Secretary of State
- [ ] 21. Draft operating agreement (LLC) or corporate bylaws (PC/Corp)
- [ ] 22. Appoint registered agent in your state (required for legal service)
- [ ] 23. File IRS Form 2553 if electing S-Corp tax status (time-sensitive: must be filed within 75 days of entity formation, or by March 15 for the prior tax year)
- [ ] 24. File any required state S-Corp election documents

**Tax and Banking Setup**
- [ ] 25. Obtain Employer Identification Number (EIN) from IRS (Form SS-4; takes minutes online)
- [ ] 26. Open dedicated business checking account (separate from personal — required for liability protection)
- [ ] 27. Open dedicated business savings account for working capital reserve and tax withholding
- [ ] 28. Register fictitious name (DBA) if your practice name differs from the entity's legal name
- [ ] 29. Obtain state and local business licenses and permits
- [ ] 30. Register with state department of revenue if your state requires sales tax registration

**Insurance Coverage**
- [ ] 31. Obtain medical malpractice insurance:
  - Compare occurrence-based vs. claims-made coverage (occurrence is more expensive but simpler)
  - Get minimum three quotes from carriers
  - Verify telehealth coverage across all states where you will practice
  - Confirm coverage for all services and procedures you will offer
  - Understand tail coverage requirements if claims-made policy
- [ ] 32. Obtain general business liability insurance
- [ ] 33. Obtain cyber liability and data breach insurance (required given PHI exposure)
- [ ] 34. Obtain workers' compensation coverage (legally required in most states once you hire employees)
- [ ] 35. Consider commercial property insurance (for physical location build-out)
- [ ] 36. Consider business interruption insurance
- [ ] 37. Consider own-occupation disability insurance (your most valuable asset is your ability to practice)

**Legal Compliance Foundations**
- [ ] 38. Review STARK Law and Anti-Kickback Statute implications with your attorney for any referral arrangements, lab relationships, or ancillary services
- [ ] 39. Prepare standard vendor contract and NDA templates for service agreements
- [ ] 40. Create a contracts tracker: every agreement, expiration date, auto-renewal clause, and termination notice window

## PHASE 2: GET CREDENTIALED
*NPI. CAQH. State license. DEA. Payers. The clock is ticking from Day 1.*

> CRITICAL TIMING: Begin this phase immediately — in parallel with entity formation. Credentialing takes 90-180 days minimum. Late start means a practice that opens with no payer contracts, generating zero insurance revenue for months. Approximately 65% of new practices open without being enrolled with all target payers because they start credentialing too late.

**Medical Licensing**
- [ ] 41. Verify your state medical license is active, current, and has no restrictions
- [ ] 42. Apply for medical licenses in any additional states where you plan to see patients
  - Check the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC) at imlcc.org — if your state participates, multi-state licensing is significantly faster
  - State licensing can take 30-180 days depending on the state
- [ ] 43. Verify board certification is current and check renewal timeline (credentialing applications require current certification)

**National Provider Identifiers (NPI)**
- [ ] 44. Apply for NPI Type 1 (individual provider identifier) through NPPES (nppes.cms.hhs.gov)
- [ ] 45. Apply for NPI Type 2 (organizational/group identifier for your practice entity) through NPPES
- [ ] 46. Verify both NPIs are active and accurate before submitting any payer applications — errors here delay everything downstream

**DEA and Controlled Substances**
- [ ] 47. Apply for DEA registration ($888 fee, 3-year period) at deadiversion.usdoj.gov
- [ ] 48. Apply for state Controlled Dangerous Substance (CDS) permit — required in most states in addition to DEA registration
- [ ] 49. If prescribing controlled substances via telehealth: obtain Electronic Prescribing for Controlled Substances (EPCS) certification — requires identity proofing and two-factor authentication
- [ ] 50. Verify state-specific controlled substance prescribing rules for telehealth in each state where you will see patients

**CAQH Profile — Do This Before Any Payer Application**
- [ ] 51. Create CAQH ProView profile (or update existing profile if you have one from prior employment)
- [ ] 52. Complete all attestation sections fully and accurately — errors, omissions, or inconsistencies restart the credentialing clock at each payer
- [ ] 53. Upload all required documents into CAQH:
  - Current malpractice certificate (showing face amount and coverage dates)
  - DEA certificate
  - State medical license(s)
  - Board certification certificate(s)
  - Current CV (include all gaps in training/employment)
  - Medical school diploma
  - Internship, residency, and fellowship certificates
  - All NPI numbers (Type 1 and Type 2)
- [ ] 54. Set a calendar reminder to re-attest your CAQH profile every 120 days (re-attestation is required; expiration suspends credentialing applications)

**Medicare and Medicaid Enrollment**
- [ ] 55. Complete Medicare enrollment through PECOS (Provider Enrollment, Chain, and Ownership System) at pecos.cms.hhs.gov
  - Allow 60-90 days for Medicare processing
  - Enroll both the individual provider (Type 1) and the practice entity (Type 2)
- [ ] 56. Complete Medicaid enrollment through your state's Medicaid agency
  - Allow 90-120 days (timelines vary significantly by state)
  - Some states require separate enrollment by county or region
- [ ] 57. Apply for CHIP (Children's Health Insurance Program) enrollment if you plan to see pediatric patients

**Commercial Payer Applications**
- [ ] 58. Identify your top 5-10 commercial payers by patient volume in your target market (analyze payer mix by zip code)
- [ ] 59. Submit applications to each target commercial payer — allow 90-120 days per payer for approval
  - Credentialing start deadline: no later than 5-6 months before your intended opening date
  - Each error or missing document restarts the clock for that payer
- [ ] 60. Track every submission: payer name, submission date, contact name at the payer, next follow-up date
- [ ] 61. Follow up with each payer weekly via phone or portal — do not wait for them to contact you
- [ ] 62. Negotiate reimbursement rates separately from credentialing — negotiate before signing any contract
- [ ] 63. Have your healthcare attorney review all payer contracts before signing — watch for carve-outs, audit provisions, and termination notice requirements
- [ ] 64. Set up payer portals for each approved payer (eligibility verification, claim status, remittance)

**Post-Credentialing Enrollment (do these once credentialing is approved)**
- [ ] 65. Enroll in Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) with each approved payer (claims submission) — allow 1-3 weeks per payer
- [ ] 66. Enroll in Electronic Remittance Advice (ERA) with each approved payer (electronic explanation of benefits) — allow 1-3 weeks
- [ ] 67. Enroll in Electronic Funds Transfer (EFT) with each approved payer (direct deposit of reimbursements) — allow 1-3 weeks

**Additional Certifications**
- [ ] 68. CLIA certificate or waiver if you plan to perform any lab tests — even simple ones like urine dipsticks (CMS application, $150-$500+ depending on certification level; allow 2-4 weeks)
- [ ] 69. State radiation license or NRC authorization if using X-ray equipment
- [ ] 70. Hospital privileges application if you plan to admit or follow patients in a hospital setting

## PHASE 3: PLAN AND SECURE FUNDING
*Financial model. Capital. Working capital reserve. Tax strategy.*

**Build the Financial Model**
- [ ] 71. Build a comprehensive 24-month financial model with your CPA:
  - Startup cost itemization (equipment, technology, legal, insurance, build-out, marketing)
  - Revenue projections by payer, CPT code, and visit volume
  - Expense projections: rent, staffing, supplies, technology, insurance, professional fees
  - Break-even analysis: calculate the exact patient visits per week to cover costs
  - Cash flow forecast — specifically model the 3-6 month gap between first patient visit and first insurance reimbursement
- [ ] 72. Model the months 2-4 working capital crisis explicitly:
  - Revenue from credentialed payers starts arriving approximately 90 days after your first billed patient visit
  - Payroll, rent, supplies, and malpractice premiums are due from Day 1
  - This gap kills practices that do not plan for it
  - Rule of thumb: calculate 6 months of full operating expenses as your pre-revenue reserve
  - Example: $30,000/month operating expenses = $180,000 minimum working capital reserve
- [ ] 73. Calculate total startup capital required (typical ranges):
  - Digital-only telehealth launch: $15,000-$50,000
  - Physical primary care practice: $70,000-$150,000 (excluding physician income replacement)
  - Physical specialty practice with procedure rooms: $150,000-$500,000+

**Secure Financing**
- [ ] 74. Explore financing options with a healthcare-focused lender:
  - SBA 7(a) loan: up to $5M, most flexible use of proceeds, 10-25 year terms
  - SBA 504 loan: for equipment and commercial real estate purchase
  - Medical practice-specific bank loans: Live Oak Bank, Bank of America Healthcare, Wells Fargo Practice Finance
  - Physician personal line of credit: use against home equity or existing assets
  - Business line of credit: target $50,000-$100,000 minimum as a cash buffer
- [ ] 75. Prepare lender submission package:
  - 3 years of personal tax returns
  - Personal financial statement (assets, liabilities, net worth)
  - Business plan with 24-month financial projections
  - CV and specialty credentials
  - Entity formation documents
- [ ] 76. Securing capital can take 2-4 months — start this in parallel with entity formation, not after

**Tax Strategy — Before You File a Single Return**
- [ ] 77. Model salary vs. distribution split with your CPA (S-Corp election advantage: distributions are not subject to self-employment tax — can save $15,000-$30,000+ per year for a physician)
- [ ] 78. Plan Section 179 equipment deductions (up to $1.16M in 2026)
- [ ] 79. Plan retirement contributions: Solo 401(k) (up to $70,000 in 2026) or SEP-IRA (up to 25% of compensation)
- [ ] 80. Establish quarterly estimated tax payment schedule (April 15, June 16, September 15, January 15)
- [ ] 81. Set up a dedicated tax reserve account: deposit estimated taxes monthly, not quarterly

**Accounting Infrastructure**
- [ ] 82. Select healthcare-specific accounting software (QuickBooks, Xero, or a practice-specific platform)
- [ ] 83. Establish chart of accounts aligned with medical revenue cycles (separate revenue by payer, expense by category)
- [ ] 84. Set up accounts receivable tracking and payer remittance reconciliation
- [ ] 85. Establish a monthly financial review cadence with your CPA (not quarterly — monthly, especially in Year 1)

## PHASE 4: LAUNCH YOUR DIGITAL CHANNELS
*EHR. Telehealth. HIPAA compliance. First patients. First revenue.*

> This is your first revenue milestone. Launch digitally before you sign a lease or hire support staff. Telehealth startup cost: $15,000-$30,000 vs. $150,000-$500,000 for a physical clinic. You prove your workflows, generate cash, and build your patient panel — before taking on physical overhead.

**Technology Selection**
- [ ] 86. Evaluate and select your EHR and Practice Management (PM) platform:
  - Evaluate minimum 3 vendors; request specialty-specific demos
  - Assess: billing integration, payer connectivity, ease of documentation, reporting capability
  - Assess: lock-in periods, exit fees, and price escalation clauses in the contract
  - Allocate 45-60 days for implementation and staff training
- [ ] 87. Select a HIPAA-compliant telehealth platform (may be integrated into your EHR or a standalone):
  - Required features: HIPAA BAA, HD video, no patient app download required, e-prescribing, secure messaging, scheduling, and payment collection
  - Verify multi-state support if you plan to see patients across state lines
- [ ] 88. Obtain Electronic Prescribing for Controlled Substances (EPCS) certification if prescribing controlled substances:
  - Requires identity proofing and two-factor authentication setup
  - Verify platform connects to major pharmacy networks (Surescripts)
- [ ] 89. Select billing software or outsourced billing service:
  - Recommendation: outsource billing at launch — reduces overhead, ensures expertise, and avoids claim errors that delay revenue
  - If in-house: procure claim scrubbing software to catch errors before submission
- [ ] 90. Identify and contract a clearinghouse for electronic claim submission

**HIPAA Compliance Program — Required Before Seeing Your First Patient**
- [ ] 91. Conduct a Security Risk Analysis (SRA) — legally required under HIPAA before handling any PHI
- [ ] 92. Draft Privacy Policies and Security Policies aligned with HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules
- [ ] 93. Identify every vendor who will touch PHI: EHR, billing service, email platform, cloud storage, telehealth platform, phone system
- [ ] 94. Execute a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with every PHI-touching vendor before going live
- [ ] 95. Designate a HIPAA Privacy Officer and HIPAA Security Officer (can be the same person; can be you)
- [ ] 96. Create a data breach response procedure (who to call, what to document, when to notify HHS)
- [ ] 97. Train all staff on HIPAA — document completion with signed acknowledgment (required annually)
- [ ] 98. Verify your HIPAA training is scheduled annually — put it on the calendar now

**Digital Infrastructure**
- [ ] 99. Set up HIPAA-compliant business email — must be encrypted at rest and in transit (standard Gmail and Outlook do not qualify without configuration)
- [ ] 100. Procure HIPAA-compliant cloud storage for records (AWS, Google Workspace with BAA, Microsoft 365 with BAA)
- [ ] 101. Set up VoIP phone system with HIPAA-compliant voicemail (voicemail messages containing PHI must be protected)
- [ ] 102. Implement cybersecurity baseline: firewall, encrypted workstations, password manager, multi-factor authentication (MFA) on all systems
- [ ] 103. Set up automatic HIPAA-compliant data backups with tested restore procedures
- [ ] 104. Set up professional email addresses on your practice domain (not personal Gmail)

**Configure for Launch**
- [ ] 105. Configure EHR: clinical templates, documentation shortcuts, problem list, medication list, charge capture codes
- [ ] 106. Set up fee schedule in billing software: confirm CPT codes, RVU-based rates, and self-pay/cash rates
- [ ] 107. Set up scheduling system: appointment types, durations, and block templates
- [ ] 108. Create digital intake forms: demographics, insurance, medical history, HIPAA acknowledgment, financial policy
- [ ] 109. Set up automated appointment reminders: 24 hours before and 1 hour before (reduces no-shows by 30-50%)
- [ ] 110. Set up patient payment portal (online payment reduces collection friction significantly)
- [ ] 111. Set up patient portal for secure messaging, lab results, and health records
- [ ] 112. Set cash-pay and out-of-network billing rates (needed for patients of payers not yet credentialed)
- [ ] 113. Set up telehealth scheduling link and verify video access from patient side

**End-to-End Testing — Before Your First Real Patient**
- [ ] 114. Schedule a test patient through the entire workflow: booking, intake forms, eligibility check, telehealth link, documentation, charge capture
- [ ] 115. Submit a test claim through the billing system and verify it processes without errors
- [ ] 116. Confirm ERA and EFT are flowing from at least one credentialed payer
- [ ] 117. Test phone, voicemail, and after-hours messaging routing
- [ ] 118. Verify prescription routing is working (send a test prescription to your own pharmacy)
- [ ] 119. Fix all identified issues before seeing a paying patient

**Digital Soft Launch**
- [ ] 120. Open scheduling and accept your first telehealth patients
- [ ] 121. Complete your first 10-20 telehealth visits to stress-test workflows
- [ ] 122. Gather explicit patient feedback after each of the first 10 visits
- [ ] 123. Identify and document any workflow bottlenecks
- [ ] 124. Verify claims are submitting and ERA/EFT is flowing for credentialed payers
- [ ] 125. Begin tracking revenue cycle KPIs from Day 1: clean claim rate, days in A/R, denial rate
- [ ] 126. Refine intake forms, scheduling templates, and documentation workflows based on real patient experience

## PHASE 5: BUILD YOUR TEAM
*Hire the right people. Onboard them properly. Build the culture.*

**Define the Team**
- [ ] 127. Define your minimum viable team for digital launch (can be very lean — many physicians launch solo or with one virtual biller)
- [ ] 128. Define your target team for physical clinic opening:
  - Front desk / patient service representative (1 per 15-20 daily patients)
  - Medical assistant or clinical support (1 per physician)
  - Billing specialist or biller (or outsource this function)
  - Practice manager (once you have 3+ staff)
- [ ] 129. Write detailed job descriptions for each role (scope of work, required credentials, reporting structure)
- [ ] 130. Research local salary ranges for each role (MGMA compensation surveys, local market data)

**Legal Setup Before First Hire**
- [ ] 131. Set up payroll processing system (ADP, Gusto, Paychex) before any employee's first day
- [ ] 132. Establish benefits package: health insurance, retirement plan contribution, PTO accrual, CME allowance for clinical staff
- [ ] 133. Draft employment agreements: at-will language, confidentiality, restrictive covenants (non-compete enforceability varies by state)
- [ ] 134. Prepare I-9, W-4, and any state-required onboarding forms

**Recruit and Hire**
- [ ] 135. Post positions on healthcare-specific job boards (Indeed Health, Practice Match, health system-affiliated career sites)
- [ ] 136. Conduct structured interviews with consistent scoring rubric
- [ ] 137. Perform background checks on all employees
- [ ] 138. Verify licenses and certifications for all clinical staff (check state licensing board, not just resume)
- [ ] 139. Check OIG Exclusion List for all employees — employing an excluded individual is a Medicare/Medicaid compliance violation with significant penalties
- [ ] 140. Perform NPDB (National Practitioner Data Bank) query on all clinical staff
- [ ] 141. Conduct DEA verification for any employed prescribers

**Onboard and Train**
- [ ] 142. Create a structured onboarding program for each role (do not wing this)
- [ ] 143. Complete mandatory HIPAA training for all staff before they access any patient information — document with signed completion records
- [ ] 144. Complete OSHA bloodborne pathogen training for all clinical staff — document with signed completion records
- [ ] 145. Train all staff on EHR/PM system (allow 1-2 full training days per staff member)
- [ ] 146. Train front desk on insurance verification, copay collection, and patient financial policy communication

**HR Infrastructure**
- [ ] 147. Create an employee handbook covering:
  - Practice mission, values, and culture
  - HIPAA privacy policies and consequences of violations
  - OSHA safety procedures
  - Workplace conduct, social media, and confidentiality policy
  - PTO accrual and usage
  - Performance review process
  - Disciplinary and termination procedures
  - Continuing education requirements
- [ ] 148. Set up performance review cadence (recommend: 30-day, 90-day, then annual)
- [ ] 149. Set up annual mandatory training schedule: HIPAA, OSHA, and any specialty-specific clinical safety training

## PHASE 6: FILL THE PATIENT SCHEDULE
*Brand. Website. Referrals. Google. Patients in the door.*

**Brand Foundation**
- [ ] 150. Choose your practice name — check: domain name availability, state medical board name restrictions, trademark conflicts, Google search conflicts
- [ ] 151. Design logo and visual identity (invest in this — your brand is the first impression for every potential patient and referrer)
- [ ] 152. Define your brand voice: warm vs. clinical, specialist vs. generalist feel — align with your specialty and your patient population

**Digital Presence**
- [ ] 153. Register your practice domain name
- [ ] 154. Build a professional, HIPAA-compliant, mobile-optimized website:
  - About the physician: training, philosophy, personal story
  - Services offered and conditions treated
  - Insurances accepted
  - How to book (online scheduling link — this is critical)
  - Contact information and location (or telehealth availability)
  - Patient portal link
  - Privacy policy page
- [ ] 155. Set up and verify Google Business Profile — this is the single highest-ROI marketing action for a new practice
  - Accurate address, hours, phone, and website
  - Upload photos of you and (when open) the practice
  - Add services and specialty keywords
- [ ] 156. Build local citation listings on major healthcare directories:
  - Healthgrades, Zocdoc, WebMD, Vitals, US News Health, Doximity
  - Claim all existing listings first — incorrect information on these sites loses patients
- [ ] 157. Implement local SEO: geography-targeted keywords, specialty-specific terms, structured data markup (schema)
- [ ] 158. Set up social media profiles on LinkedIn (professional credibility) and any specialty-relevant platforms
- [ ] 159. List practice on telehealth marketplaces if offering virtual care: Zocdoc, Sesame, direct specialty directories

**Reputation and Content**
- [ ] 160. Establish a process for requesting reviews from satisfied patients (via Healthgrades, Google — make it easy with a direct link)
- [ ] 161. Create a protocol for responding to all online reviews — positive and negative, professionally, within 48 hours
- [ ] 162. Create a content plan: 1-2 specialty-specific patient education blog posts per month builds SEO and trust
- [ ] 163. Set up Google Alerts for your name and practice name

**Referral Network**
- [ ] 164. Identify the top 10-20 referring physicians and care coordinators in your target geography
- [ ] 165. Schedule introductory meetings with key referrers before you open — not after
- [ ] 166. Create a referral kit: one-page practice overview, referral form or electronic referral link, direct scheduling contact
- [ ] 167. Send a formal opening announcement to your full referral network on opening day (or 2 weeks before)
- [ ] 168. Join the local medical society — referral relationships are built in person
- [ ] 169. Attend hospital medical staff meetings even without inpatient privileges — network presence matters
- [ ] 170. Build relationships with hospital case managers and discharge planners (major outpatient referral source)
- [ ] 171. Set up a referral tracking system: know which referrers send patients and follow up with clinical feedback after each referral visit

**Patient Acquisition**
- [ ] 172. Set up automated new patient welcome email sequence (confirmation, forms, what to expect)
- [ ] 173. Consider targeted Google Ads for local patient acquisition — high ROI for new practices with no existing reputation
- [ ] 174. Consider specialty-relevant community outreach: health fairs, hospital-sponsored screenings, community talks, workplace wellness programs
- [ ] 175. Establish a patient referral program for established patients (appropriate for your specialty and ethics rules)

## PHASE 7: OPEN YOUR CLINIC DOORS
*Location. Lease. Build-out. Equipment. OSHA. Grand opening.*

> Start this phase after your digital practice is generating consistent revenue and your workflows are proven. Your lease negotiation is stronger with real revenue data. Your build-out is de-risked because you know which services patients actually want.

**Site Selection**
- [ ] 176. Define your geographic target area using demographic and competitive analysis:
  - Target patient population density
  - Competitor density by specialty
  - Insurance coverage rates by zip code
  - Proximity to referral sources and hospital systems
  - Drive-time analysis: most patients travel under 15 minutes to a primary care visit
- [ ] 177. Engage a commercial real estate broker who specializes in medical offices — they know how to negotiate tenant improvement allowances and HIPAA-compliant build-outs
- [ ] 178. Evaluate each candidate site against these criteria:
  - Minimum square footage for your specialty (solo: 1,000-2,500 sq ft; 2 providers: 2,500-4,000 sq ft)
  - Parking: minimum 4 spaces per exam room, ADA-accessible
  - ADA compliance for entrance, hallways, and restrooms
  - Proximity to complementary providers (lab, imaging, pharmacy)
  - Existing medical office buildout (saves $50,000-$150,000 vs. raw space)
  - Zoning: confirm medical use is permitted before negotiating

**Lease Negotiation — Protect Yourself Before You Sign**
- [ ] 179. Negotiate lease terms (have your healthcare attorney in the room):
  - Tenant Improvement Allowance (TIA): target $30-$80/sq ft for a new buildout
  - Rent abatement during build-out: target 2-6 months of free rent
  - Personal guarantee cap: limit to 12-18 months of rent, not the full lease term
  - Sublease and assignment rights
  - Renewal options with rate caps or fixed increases
  - Right to terminate without penalty if payer credentialing falls through
  - HVAC, plumbing, and structural maintenance responsibility clearly defined
- [ ] 180. Have healthcare attorney review the full lease before signing — a standard office lease has clauses that create significant liability for medical practices
- [ ] 181. Sign lease and obtain Certificate of Occupancy timeline from landlord

**Facility Design and Build-Out**
- [ ] 182. Hire an architect or medical office designer for space planning (especially if new build or major renovation)
- [ ] 183. Design for patient flow: parking to entrance to check-in to waiting to rooming to exam to checkout — minimize backtracking
- [ ] 184. Plan exam room count for projected patient volume: 1 exam room supports 15-20 patients per day
- [ ] 185. Design HIPAA-compliant registration desk: patients cannot see other patients' information at check-in
- [ ] 186. Designate separate clean and soiled utility areas per OSHA and state health department standards
- [ ] 187. Design ADA-compliant restrooms (minimum one per gender)
- [ ] 188. Plan keypad or biometric access to clinical supply storage and medication storage
- [ ] 189. Get minimum 3 contractor bids from licensed general contractors with medical office build-out experience
- [ ] 190. Pull all required building permits before construction starts
- [ ] 191. Manage build-out timeline: set milestone schedule and hold contractor to it

**Equipment Procurement**
- [ ] 192. Source exam tables for each exam room (new or refurbished — refurbished is fine for non-critical items)
- [ ] 193. Procure specialty-specific diagnostic equipment (otoscope/ophthalmoscope sets, blood pressure cuffs, EKG machine, etc.)
- [ ] 194. Procure sterilization equipment if performing procedures
- [ ] 195. Order waiting room furniture, reception desk, and staff workstations
- [ ] 196. Order clinical workstations for each exam room (wall-mounted or cart-based)
- [ ] 197. Order initial clinical supplies inventory: target 30-day supply on hand at opening
- [ ] 198. Join a group purchasing organization (GPO) to reduce ongoing supply costs by 15-30%
- [ ] 199. Establish ongoing vendor relationships and reorder alert thresholds

**Physical IT and Communications**
- [ ] 200. Install business-grade internet with a redundant backup circuit (do not open without internet redundancy)
- [ ] 201. Run ethernet cabling to all exam rooms and staff workstations (physical hardwire preferred over WiFi for HIPAA reliability)
- [ ] 202. Install clinical workstations in all exam rooms and staff areas
- [ ] 203. Install HIPAA-compliant physical phone system with exam room extensions and after-hours routing
- [ ] 204. Install physical security: keypad locks on clinical areas, security cameras at entrance and parking
- [ ] 205. Verify all records storage is locked and access-controlled

**OSHA Compliance — Required Before First In-Person Patient**
- [ ] 206. Create a written Exposure Control Plan for bloodborne pathogens (required by OSHA for all healthcare facilities)
- [ ] 207. Document sharps disposal procedures
- [ ] 208. Install sharps containers in all clinical areas and exam rooms
- [ ] 209. Stock PPE in all clinical areas: gloves (multiple sizes), surgical masks, N95s, eye protection, gowns
- [ ] 210. Install biohazard waste disposal system: contract with licensed medical waste vendor
- [ ] 211. Document chemical safety protocols: SDS (Safety Data Sheet) binders for all chemicals
- [ ] 212. Train all clinical staff on OSHA bloodborne pathogen standards — document with signed completion sheets
- [ ] 213. Schedule annual OSHA refresher training and put it on the calendar now
- [ ] 214. Conduct a mock OSHA inspection before opening (walk through the checklist yourself)

**Physical Workflow Integration**
- [ ] 215. Design in-person patient intake workflow: arrival, check-in, HIPAA form confirmation, insurance verification, copay collection, rooming
- [ ] 216. Update EHR templates for in-person encounter types: physical exams, procedure notes, preventive visit documentation
- [ ] 217. Integrate telehealth and in-person scheduling into a single unified calendar view
- [ ] 218. Test the full in-person workflow end-to-end with a staff member before opening day
- [ ] 219. Plan a soft opening: see patients by appointment for 1-2 weeks before announcing grand opening

---

## PHASE 8: PROTECT YOUR CASH
*Revenue cycle. Billing. Collections. Cash flow discipline.*

> This phase starts the day you see your first patient — and never ends. Revenue cycle discipline is what determines whether the practice survives the first two years.

**Billing Configuration**
- [ ] 220. Confirm all payer EDI, ERA, and EFT enrollments are active before billing a single claim
- [ ] 221. Verify fee schedule is loaded correctly in billing software: all CPT codes, modifiers, and place-of-service codes for your specialty
- [ ] 222. Enable real-time eligibility verification before every patient visit — not the morning of, not at check-in: at scheduling and again at check-in
- [ ] 223. Set up prior authorization workflow for high-authorization-requirement specialties (imaging, surgery, injections, infusions)
- [ ] 224. Enable claim scrubbing before submission to catch errors before they become denials
- [ ] 225. Load telehealth-specific billing requirements if offering virtual visits: modifier 95, Place of Service code 10, GT modifier as applicable by payer

**Revenue Cycle Monitoring (Run These Weekly)**
- [ ] 226. Track clean claim rate weekly — target: greater than 95% (below 90% is a billing process problem)
- [ ] 227. Track first-pass resolution rate — target: greater than 90%
- [ ] 228. Monitor days in accounts receivable — target: under 30 days for most specialties
- [ ] 229. Monitor net collection rate — target: greater than 95%
- [ ] 230. Review denial rate by payer and by CPT code weekly
- [ ] 231. Review A/R aging report monthly: watch the 90-day and 120-day buckets — claims past 120 days are approaching appeal deadline

**Denial Management**
- [ ] 232. Categorize every denial as clinical or administrative and log it
- [ ] 233. Appeal every denial within the payer's deadline — typically 90-180 days from denial date; missing this date forfeits the claim
- [ ] 234. Track appeal win rate — a win rate below 50% means your appeals are not being written correctly
- [ ] 235. Identify denial patterns: if the same CPT code is being denied by the same payer repeatedly, the root cause is either your documentation, your coding, or the payer's policy
- [ ] 236. Fix root causes immediately — denials that repeat are denials that compound

**Patient Collections**
- [ ] 237. Collect copays and known coinsurance at every visit, at check-in — no exceptions
- [ ] 238. Provide patients with good faith estimates of expected costs before service (required under the No Surprises Act)
- [ ] 239. Offer online payment portal: patients pay faster when they can pay online at 10pm
- [ ] 240. Offer payment plans for balances over $500 (reduces write-offs)
- [ ] 241. Communicate financial policy in writing at first visit: what you charge, when you charge it, and what happens with unpaid balances
- [ ] 242. Send balance statements within 30 days of claim adjudication
- [ ] 243. Define and document your write-off policy and approval thresholds (unwritten policies become inconsistent policies)

**Cash Flow Discipline**
- [ ] 244. Generate a weekly cash flow report: cash in, cash out, upcoming obligations
- [ ] 245. Maintain a 13-week rolling cash flow forecast — know exactly when money arrives and when it leaves, 3 months ahead
- [ ] 246. Review actual vs. projected revenue and expenses quarterly
- [ ] 247. Calculate payroll-to-revenue ratio monthly — target: under 35% (above 40% is unsustainable)
- [ ] 248. Review payer contract rates annually and negotiate increases using your actual utilization data — most practices overpay or undercharge because they never renegotiate
- [ ] 249. Conduct a formal financial review with your CPA quarterly

## PHASE 9: RUN AND GROW
*Operations. Compliance. Optimize. Scale.*

**Post-Launch Operations**
- [ ] 250. Conduct a 30-day operational review: what is working, what is not, what needs to be changed immediately
- [ ] 251. Conduct a 90-day financial health review:
  - Actual revenue vs. projected
  - Actual expenses vs. projected
  - Current cash balance and runway
  - A/R aging and collection rate
  - Payroll-to-revenue ratio
- [ ] 252. Track patient satisfaction scores — use Google reviews, Healthgrades, and optional internal surveys
- [ ] 253. Track no-show rate — target: under 10%; if above 15%, implement a cancellation policy with a deposit
- [ ] 254. Track time-to-appointment: target under 10 days for established patients, under 21 days for new patients
- [ ] 255. Track staff turnover — a turnover rate above 25% annually signals a compensation or culture problem

**Compliance — Annual and Ongoing**
- [ ] 256. Conduct annual HIPAA training for all staff — document completion (this is an audit requirement)
- [ ] 257. Re-conduct Security Risk Analysis annually — required by HIPAA Security Rule
- [ ] 258. Audit all Business Associate Agreements annually — verify none have expired or failed to auto-renew
- [ ] 259. Conduct annual OSHA bloodborne pathogen refresher training — document completion
- [ ] 260. Audit CLIA compliance and proficiency testing documentation (if performing lab tests)
- [ ] 261. Audit DEA controlled substance logs quarterly — verify counts, verify destruction records
- [ ] 262. Audit patient privacy notice distribution at intake (are patients still receiving and signing the HIPAA notice?)
- [ ] 263. Review payer policy updates annually — CPT code changes, coverage policy changes, and documentation requirements change every year
- [ ] 264. Renew DEA registration every 3 years — set a calendar reminder 90 days before expiration
- [ ] 265. Renew state medical license on the state board's cycle — typically every 2 years
- [ ] 266. Complete CME requirements for board certification renewal

**Growth and Scale**
- [ ] 267. At 12 months: evaluate adding ancillary services (in-house lab, imaging, physical therapy, wellness programs) — model the revenue and compliance implications before adding
- [ ] 268. At 12 months: renegotiate payer contract rates using real utilization and quality data — most practices get better rates on renewal than initial contract
- [ ] 269. At 18-24 months: evaluate adding an advanced practice provider (PA or NP) to increase patient capacity
- [ ] 270. At 18-24 months: evaluate adding a second physician — define the partnership or employment structure with your attorney before recruiting
- [ ] 271. Expand telehealth to additional states via IMLC as demand grows beyond your primary market
- [ ] 272. Evaluate Direct Primary Care (DPC) or concierge tier addition if your panel supports a subscription model
- [ ] 273. Plan for next location or group practice expansion if you are consistently at capacity for 90+ days

## THE THREE MISTAKES THAT DESTROY MOST PRACTICES

These are not hypothetical. They are the three most preventable financial catastrophes in independent physician practice formation:

**Mistake 1: Wrong entity structure or wrong timing of incorporation**
Cost: $200,000 to $400,000 in avoidable taxes
Prevention: Step 19 — consult healthcare attorney AND CPA before filing any formation documents

**Mistake 2: Starting credentialing too late**
Cost: $100,000 to $450,000 in lost or delayed revenue
Prevention: Steps 41-70 — start credentialing on Day 1, in parallel with everything else

**Mistake 3: Underestimating the working capital requirement**
Cost: practice closure, personal financial crisis
Prevention: Steps 71-73 — model the months 2-4 cash flow crisis explicitly before you spend a dollar
