## The Three Paths

### Path 1 — Accept the PE Partnership at Northwest ENT

**What this looks like:**

You become a named partner in the PE entity. Your compensation shifts from associate salary to a base + partnership distributions. The catch: PE ownership means a layer of extraction sits between your clinical production and your take-home. Founding physicians capture dividend distributions; your equity is diluted relative to what a traditional partnership would offer.

**Financial picture (Atlanta ENT, 7-physician PE-backed group):**

| Year | Estimated Compensation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (partnership) | $420K–$480K | Base + initial distributions; PE takes ~20–30% of practice margin before distributions reach partners |
| Year 3 | $450K–$520K | Modest growth if practice revenue grows; PE exit event possible (sale to larger group) |
| Year 5 | $470K–$560K or exit payout | If PE exits (sells practice), partnership stake may generate a one-time event — but typically diluted vs. what founder-physicians received |

**What you own:** A minority equity stake in a PE-controlled entity. You do not control decisions about billing rates, staffing, or whether the practice is sold.

**What you don't own:** Your patient relationships — most PE contracts include non-compete and patient list restrictions if you leave.

**The honest question to ask before signing:**
> "What percentage of the practice do I own, what did the founding physicians receive when they sold, and what is my projected payout if the PE firm exits in 3–5 years?"

### Path 2 — Move to a Different Practice (Non-PE or Physician-Owned)

**What this looks like:**

You leave Northwest ENT for a practice with better economics — either a physician-owned group where you join as a true partner, or a hospital employment model in a health system (EMORY, Piedmont, Wellstar) where the tradeoff is stability and benefits over ownership.

**Financial picture:**

| Structure | Year 1 | Year 3 | Year 5 | Ownership |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Physician-owned group (true partnership track) | $380K–$440K | $480K–$580K | $520K–$640K + equity | Real equity, physician-controlled |
| Hospital employment (EMORY/Piedmont) | $420K–$500K | $450K–$530K | $470K–$550K + RVU bonuses | None — but no overhead either |

**The tradeoff:**
Physician-owned group gives you a genuine ownership path but requires navigating another 2–3 year track and a new non-compete exposure. Hospital employment trades financial upside for stability and removes the business management burden entirely.

**Key question before pursuing:**
> "Does this practice have a buy-in requirement? What have the last three physicians who made partner paid, and what did they receive?"

### Path 3 — Go Independent (Solo or Small Group)

**What this looks like:**

You form your own practice — solo or with one partner — in Atlanta. You own your patient relationships, your revenue, and your decisions. You also own the setup, the overhead, and the patient acquisition problem.

**Financial picture:**

| Year | Gross Collections | Overhead (55–65%) | Net Income | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | $600K–$750K | $390K–$490K | $210K–$360K | Credentialing delays and ramp-up reduce first-year collections significantly |
| Year 2 | $800K–$950K | $440K–$520K | $360K–$430K | Practice stabilises; referral base established |
| Year 3 | $900K–$1.1M | $450K–$550K | $450K–$550K | Full ramp; strong SEO/review base driving inbound |
| Year 5 | $1.0M–$1.3M | $480K–$580K | $520K–$720K | If you add a PA or NP, revenue scales without proportional overhead |

**The honest risk in Year 1:**
Your income drops before it rises. Credentialing takes 90–120 days — you cannot bill insurance until it clears. You need 3–6 months of operating capital before you open. Most physicians who attempt independence and fail do so in months 3–5, not because the practice wasn't viable but because they ran out of cash waiting for insurance payments.

**What you're buying:**
Full ownership of your patient relationships. No non-compete clock resetting. The ability to optimize for the patient mix you want (high-value, recurring cases — not earwax cleaning). Full agency on pricing, staffing, and practice direction.

## If You Choose Path 3: The 5 Decisions in Order

These are the decisions that determine whether an independent ENT practice in Atlanta succeeds in year 1. They must be made in this sequence — getting the order wrong is expensive.

**Decision 1 — Entity and incorporation (Month -12 to -9)**

Form your PLLC or PC before you give notice. Do not wait until after you leave. The entity formation clock starts here, and the tax structure you choose determines your personal income for the next decade. If you incorporate after leaving, you delay everything downstream and pay personal income tax rates on your first months of revenue.

*Key question: S-Corp election or standard PLLC? Your accountant should model both.*

**Decision 2 — Credentialing sequence (Month -9 to open)**

Submit payer enrollment applications the day your entity is formed — not the day you open. Credentialing takes 90–120 days per payer. Atlanta's major payers: BCBS, Aetna, UHC, Cigna, Humana, Medicaid, Medicare. You need all of them live on the day you see your first insured patient. Missing this by even 4 weeks costs you 4 weeks of billable revenue — at Atlanta ENT rates, that's $30K–$60K.

*Key question: Do you use a credentialing consultant or handle it yourself?*

**Decision 3 — Patient acquisition infrastructure (Month -6 to open)**

This is your stated #1 challenge and it applies whether you go independent or not. For Atlanta ENT, the three channels that actually drive new patients:

| Channel | What to build before you open | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| Google / SEO | Claim and optimize Google Business profile; build a practice website with specialty-specific content (chronic sinusitis, sleep apnea, hearing loss — not generic ENT page) | Start 6 months before opening |
| Online reviews | Before you open, ask 10–15 colleagues and referring physicians to leave a Google review of their experience working with you. Aim for 20+ reviews before your first public patient. | Start 3 months before opening |
| Insurance directories | Ensure your profile is complete and showing "accepting new patients" on every payer directory — this is the #1 channel for new patients searching by insurance | Live on day 1 of credentialing approval |

*Key question: Which subspecialty do you want to be known for in Atlanta? Your SEO strategy depends on this.*

**Decision 4 — Working capital (Month -6)**

You need 4–5 months of operating capital before your first insurance payment arrives. At your overhead level:

| Monthly operating cost estimate | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent (medical office, Atlanta) | $4,000–$8,000/mo |
| Staff (1 MA, 1 front desk, 1 biller) | $12,000–$18,000/mo |
| Malpractice insurance | $1,500–$2,500/mo |
| EMR + billing software | $500–$1,500/mo |
| Supplies and misc | $1,000–$2,000/mo |
| **Total monthly burn** | **$19,000–$32,000/mo** |
| **4-month capital needed** | **$76,000–$128,000** |

*Key question: Do you have this in liquid savings, or do you need a business line of credit?*

**Decision 5 — EMR selection (Month -3)**

For Atlanta ENT: athenahealth, Modernizing Medicine (ModMed ENT module), or DrChrono are the most common. The choice matters less than the timing — you need it configured, staff trained, and payer billing rules loaded before your first patient, not after.

*Key question: What EMR are your referring PCPs and hospital colleagues using? Interoperability makes referrals easier.*
